When the Output Looks Like Learning

Generative AI has flooded schools with polished academic products, hollowing out the effortful cognitive processing essential to genuine understanding. It is time to move beyond detection and prohibition. We must design for Pedagogical Friction.

Core Concepts

To analyze the educational shift under generative AI, we must define the symbolic and cognitive components of the learning environment.

Symbolic Production

The act of creating representations through sign systems. Translating internal, partial, or tentative mental models into external forms (writing sentences, sketching diagrams, solving proofs) that can be shared, revised, and evaluated.

K-12 Example: A student writes an original paragraph explaining photosynthesis in their own words.

Symbolic Environment

The total set of signs, texts, and representations within which learners work. Includes textbooks, classroom discussions, slides, search results, and AI outputs. It shapes what counts as a credible, competent, or finished piece of academic work.

K-12 Example: The collection of sources, peer feedback, and model essays a student references during a research unit.

Cognitive Bypass

The state in which the symbolic output of an assignment is generated without the effortful cognitive processing the assignment was designed to require. Distinct from cheating; this is a learning category, not an ethical rule infraction.

K-12 Example: Outsource writing a history essay to Claude, producing a highly polished product without wrestling with the historical arguments.

Ethical Anchors: C.O.R.E. & H.E.A.R.T.

Complementary frameworks developed by Micah Miner to guide ethical, human-centered AI integration and preserve active learning dispositions.

C.O.R.E. Framework

Cognitive & Civic Anchors
C
Critical Thinking

Analyzing issues from multiple angles, challenging assumptions, and interrogating AI-generated outputs by comparing them with primary sources.

O
Openness

Treating AI outputs as starting points rather than final answers; layering automated summaries with diverse human perspectives.

R
Respect

Fostering empathy, civility, and equitable participation during digital discussions and AI-mediated tasks.

E
Engagement

Encouraging active participation, relationship-building, and using interactive tools to connect deeply with historical and civic content.

H.E.A.R.T. Framework

Ethical & Emotional Dimensions
H
Honesty

Transparently questioning AI authenticity, acknowledging uncertainties, and identifying algorithmic biases or errors.

E
Empathy

Ensuring that technology does not displace genuine human interaction and valuing multiple perspectives in discussion.

A
Accountability

Maintaining academic integrity and understanding the social and cognitive implications of algorithmic decisions.

R
Responsibility

Using technology ethically and actively resisting frictionless consumption by choosing to wrestle with ideas.

T
Thoughtfulness

Reflecting on the long-term ethical implications of technology and its impact on human dignity.

From Orality to Tertiary Algorithmicity

Drawing on Walter Ong's media ecology framework, we trace how technology shapes symbolic production and cognitive habits of mind.

Stage 1

Primary Orality

Communication occurs exclusively through spoken words in cultures with no knowledge of writing. Thought is communal, aggregates concepts, is situationally oriented, and relies heavily on rhythmic, memorable formulas to preserve ideas.

  • Noetic Base: Human memory constraints shape structure.
  • Production: Utterances are immediate, impermanent, and dialogic.
  • Knower/Known: Ideas are tightly fused with the speaker.
Human Mind
(Internal Memory Storage)
↓
Spoken Utterance
(Immediate & Fluid Expression)
Stage 2

Literacy (Chirography & Typography)

The introduction of handwritten script and print media enables thoughts to be frozen in physical space. Literacy externalizes memory, allowing systematic comparison, abstract analysis, and the cumulative building of institutional knowledge.

  • Noetic Base: Separation of the knower from the known.
  • Production: Writing serves as a silent, solitary thinking process.
  • Cognitive Effect: Empowers abstract logic and linear argumentation.
Human Mind
(Internal Schema Formulations)
↓
Written/Printed Text
(Solitary, Solved in Ink or Print)
Stage 3

Secondary Orality

Electronic media (telephone, television, radio) reintroduces oral modes of speech and communal immediacy, but this orality is fundamentally structured and sustained by the underlying habits of a literate culture (scripts, Teleprompters).

  • Noetic Base: Spontaneous community-building via broadcast.
  • Production: Human-authored texts spoken dynamically.
  • Cognitive Effect: Combines immediacy of voice with literate planning.
Human Author
(Written Script/Structure)
↓
Electronic Broadcast
(Amplified Voice/Mediated Speech)
Stage 4 (New)

Tertiary Algorithmicity

Algorithmic systems shift from mediating, cataloging, or curation to generating symbolic content autonomously. The technology actively outputs language, argument, and art, while humans act primarily as prompt-givers and consumers.

Noetic Displacement

Externalization of core thinking processes like synthesis and evaluation.

Rhetorical Saturation

A flood of highly competent, uniform, and low-friction prose.

Existential Abstraction

Bypassing the discomfort of struggle, losing "presence" in learning.

Infrastructural Consolidation

Dependence on standard, opaque corporate platform models.

Human Prompt
(Minimal Cognitive Demand)
↓
LLM / Algorithmic Engine
(Autonomous Symbolic Production)

The Paradox of Unproductive Success

When a student achieves a high-performance score but bypasses the effortful cognitive processing required to construct durable schemas in memory.

Learning Outcome Performance Outcome
High / High

Productive Success

Perfect alignment. The student performs well and achieves deep cognitive growth.

Low / High

Unproductive Success

The AI trap. High performance outcome, but cognitive work is bypassed.

High / Low

Productive Failure

Wrestling with difficulty. Low initial score, but builds robust schemas.

Low / Low

Unproductive Failure

Disengagement. The student fails to perform and fails to process content.

Unproductive Success AI Threat Vector

Unproductive success occurs when a student delivers an excellent final artifact (e.g., a well-crafted essay, correct coding syntax, or a polished slide deck) but does so by outsourcing the reasoning to an algorithm. Because the student bypasses cognitive tension, they construct no mental schemas in long-term memory.

Performance Output

Advanced level structure, rich prose, accurate references, clear layout.

Internal Schema

Null. No retrieval strength is established, and no active conceptual reorganization occurs.

The Pedagogical Friction Framework

A design matrix for K–12 educators to preserve effortful cognitive struggle in four key instructional dimensions.

Noetic Friction

Cognitive Load
Frictionless Bypass

AI generates summaries and analyses directly; the student reads the polished answer without encountering cognitive conflict or gaps in their own schema.

Friction Preserved

Students must formulate and document initial hypotheses, make predictions, and struggle to outline relationships before using any AI tools for verification.

Cognitive Target

Ensures that the mental processing (germane cognitive load) required for schema acquisition takes place in the student's brain, not in an external model.

Design Strategy: Visible Thinking Inversions

Instead of assessing a final product, grade the evolution of the student's conceptual model. Require students to submit a "thinking log" showing the shift from an initial diagram (pre-AI) to a refined analysis (post-AI verification).

Rhetorical Friction

Expression & Writing
Frictionless Bypass

Prompt-engineering replaces draft composition. The writer uses the AI to output paragraphs, bypassing the struggle of finding words and discovering ideas through writing.

Friction Preserved

Writing is treated as an epistemic act. Assignments require localized data, personal narratives, and interactive, live dialogical defenses of claims.

Cognitive Target

Preserves writing as a method of discovery. Writers formulate, discard, and restructure concepts while struggling to translate thoughts into signs.

Design Strategy: Situated Writing & Dialogic Defense

Build assignments around local events, school history, or personal observations that lack public online data. Integrate oral explanations, peer workshops, or 3-minute oral defenses to anchor the author to the text.

Existential Friction

Disposition & Vulnerability
Frictionless Bypass

Students receive immediate, clean answers to questions, removing the discomfort of confusion, the vulnerability of not knowing, and the need for patience.

Friction Preserved

The classroom norms value not-knowing as a necessary developmental stage. Inquiry paths are designed to keep students in a state of productive ambiguity.

Cognitive Target

Builds intellectual character, tolerance for ambiguity, curiosity, and epistemic agency (seeing oneself as a primary thinker, not just an information retriever).

Design Strategy: Inquiry Before Resolution

Create activities where students are given complex, uncurated data points and must sit with the mystery for a set duration before seeking summaries. Instructors model intellectual vulnerability by sharing their own struggle.

Infrastructural Friction

Policy & Equity
Exclusionary Friction

Creating barriers that block access for students (e.g. banning tools that assist English learners, or penalizing students based on faulty AI detector scans).

Productive Friction

Designing challenges that drive active thinking (e.g. allowing translation aids but preserving the cognitive demand for conceptual synthesis).

Systemic Target

Aligns grading systems, pacing guides, and technological deployments to incentivize thinking effort rather than product volume or surface polish.

Design Strategy: Equity-First Accommodations

Distinguish when AI functions as an accessibility accommodation (like speech-to-text for motor-skill difficulties) versus when it displaces the primary cognitive construct being evaluated (like writing the essay itself).
